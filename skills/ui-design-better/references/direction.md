# Direction and composition

Read for a new visual identity, significant redesign, or explicit comparison. For an established direction, use only the affected parts.

## References as evidence

First distinguish three jobs for a reference: inspiration, a quality benchmark, or precise reproduction. These call for different kinds of fidelity.

For product references, inspect a small set of strong category peers. Describe visible decisions and their reasons: content order, composition, scale, type, palette, imagery, icons, wording, and use context. “Clean, spacious, one accent” is too generic to explain a design. Separate:

- familiar conventions that reduce learning;
- habitual structures that could be improved;
- identifying features that should not be copied as a bundle.

For interaction references, inspect the relevant path: trigger, affected object, feedback, next state, and failure/recovery. A visual screenshot does not establish how an unseen interaction works. Record whether evidence came from operation, a recording, a page description, or memory. When unavailable, mark the affected claims as unverified rather than describing imagined evidence.

Transfer relationships and mechanisms, not another product's names, business modules, skin, or content. Deliberate absences matter: a reference with no explanatory header is not automatically missing one. Retain category conventions when they help; novelty must repay its learning cost.

## Tone with observable consequences

Useful axes include energy (quiet–loud), finish (raw–precise), density (sparse–dense), weight (light–heavy), and formality (playful–solemn). Choose from audience, subject, brand, and setting. These axes are independent: a quiet product can have a daring composition; dense work can be refined.

Translate adjectives into decisions:

| Intention | Decisions that make it assessable |
| --- | --- |
| Refined | Alignment tolerance, optical spacing, edge treatment, restrained variation, resolved states |
| Restrained | What receives emphasis, what stays still, which material or hue is allowed to dominate |
| Monumental | Scale relative to the viewport, image occupancy, contrast and reading distance |
| Literary | Typeface character, measure, leading, prose cadence, relationship of text to image |
| Raw | Specific misregistration, coarse texture, blunt edges or abrupt cuts, used intentionally |
| Simple | The necessary information and decisions made easier; explicit omissions |

Do not automatically translate “good design” into warm beige, soft shadows, and wide gaps. Do not force noise, torn edges, or neon onto a product whose evidence calls for calm. If the brief fixes the tone, vary solutions within it rather than inserting an unrelated safe option.

## Generate concepts from a mechanism

These are optional engines, not style presets:

- a material interacting with its environment, informing light, edges, depth, and resistance;
- a specific scene with a place, time, and rhythm;
- a product personality expressed through type, language, and movement;
- a relevant visual tradition or design movement, borrowing its grammar rather than ornaments;
- another medium's organizing principle, borrowing a few powerful relationships;
- a deliberate change to a familiar proportion, reading order, or operation.

Also start directly from what the product handles: a sample, layer, route, file, sound, decision, or transformation. A metaphor should produce useful interface decisions, not a cumbersome prop replica. Do not repeatedly reach for receipts, cassette players, old terminals, or other fashionable objects without a product reason.

When ideation is stuck, changing the source of the concept or inviting a fresh perspective can help. Neither a fixed candidate count nor multiple agents is required. Do not anchor every candidate to the same reference and rename the results.

## Color as identity and semantics

Start with brand assets, the actual product, or its content. A metaphor's literal color is one possibility, not an obligation: wood need not force every unrelated product into brown.

Notice the category's default palette and reference palettes before choosing. Explore another hue family, value relationship, or monochrome approach when it reveals a useful alternative. Establish background, text, action, selected surface, border, disabled, and semantic state roles. A coherent one-color or two-color system is complete; content can carry the chromatic richness.

If comparing directions, “light, dark, warm” alone is weak differentiation. Give each a coherent color identity or a clearly different composition. Brand requirements override a desire to make palettes different.

## Structure before ornament

Use a quick grayscale structure when hierarchy is unclear. Indicate the objects, relative areas, reading entry, leading action, and feedback. Show before/after states if the interaction changes the structure. Add another device sketch only when input or layout materially differs. This is a reasoning aid, not a mandatory wireframe deliverable.

For persuasive pages, consider a full image with a safe text area, a single centered object, the product itself as the interface, typography as the picture, a full-width stacked composition, a collage/grid, vertical or diagonal flow, or a split layout. These are openings into a larger design space. A split layout can be excellent when its media has enough presence; a tiny screenshot opposite a large headline seldom carries equal weight.

For workspaces and content browsing, structure around the operation: list–detail, canvas–inspector, timeline, spatial map, comparison, or another domain-appropriate surface. Keep the useful content in view. A hero slogan, repeated logo row, or oversized toolbar should earn the space it occupies. An activity banner should not bury the first useful row.

Real content constrains structure; do not squeeze it into a preconceived skeleton. Conversely, do not let a generic headline–paragraph–two-buttons block silently determine every composition. Resolve content and structure together.

## Compare fairly, then commit

Choose an exposing slice: a complex form product needs a form, not only an attractive cover. Build representative previews rather than multiple complete implementations. A neutral comparison surface can place them side by side, with a way to inspect each at actual size. Live previews are helpful; screenshots are acceptable when labeled accurately. Candidate selection controls do not themselves communicate a choice back to an agent unless that mechanism exists.

Hold product facts, user task, data, viewport, and asset quality comparable. For a layout experiment, hold visual identity fixed. For an identity comparison, vary more than hue: composition, type, image logic, control language, or the visual expression of the core action. Shared task semantics need not change. Do not handicap a candidate to make the recommendation win.

Different directions should also test different memorable moments, where the task permits. If they share the same operation, preserve that useful invariant and find distinction in how its object, result, and surrounding composition are expressed; moving the same button a few pixels is not a new interaction concept.

Look at silhouettes and value masses with labels hidden or at thumbnail size. If the alternatives have the same visual center and structure, question whether they test anything new. Compare to category peers for accidental imitation as well. Different wording is not a different idea.

Select for task fit, recognizability, content capacity, readability, feasibility, and taste. Record what the user likes, dislikes, and wants preserved. If combining ideas, restate the resulting intent and test its coherence rather than assembling favorite parts without a governing relationship.

## The memorable moment

Concentrate craft where it has leverage: direct manipulation, a first meaningful result, completion, waiting, an empty state, or a quiet corner such as the footer. In a tool, the distinctive feature may be unusually legible business relationships rather than visual spectacle.

Show the result where it happens. Express meaningful quantities as area, length, count, or another truthful encoding. Give controls a plausible weight or resistance when it improves feedback. Make AI work visible through actual progress and result changes, not invented percentages or a generic spinner. Carry a chosen visual grammar into related icons and states without repeating the same composition everywhere.

## Feasibility check

Before broad implementation, verify audience and brand fit, a credible action-to-result path, obtainable media, reasonable production cost, device viability, and sufficient distinction from references. An ambitious technique is welcome when its payoff and fallback are understood. If the essential premise cannot be delivered, revise the premise or explicitly narrow the deliverable.
